Slide 1

\textbf{Duality - Interpretations}

\begin{align*}
\text{max } \sum_{i=1}^n c_i x_i &= c^T x \\
\text{s.t. } \quad &AX \le b \\
&x \ge 0
\end{align*}
\begin{tikzpicture}[->,>=stealth',shorten >=1pt,auto,node distance=2cm,
    thick]
    \node[draw,red,rounded corners] (primal) at (2,2) {};
    \node[draw,red,rounded corners] (dual) at (2,-2) {};
    \node[left of=primal,node distance=1.5cm] (dual_label) {Dual};
    \draw[red,thick] (dual_label.east) to[out=90,in=180] (primal.west);
    \draw[red,thick] (dual_label.east) to[out=0,in=270] (dual.west);
\end{tikzpicture}

\begin{align*}
\text{min } \sum_{j=1}^m b_j y_j &= b^T y \\
\text{s.t. } \quad &A^T y \ge c \\
&y \ge 0
\end{align*}

\textbf{Description:}

This slide introduces the concept of duality in linear programming.  It shows the primal problem (maximization) and its corresponding dual problem (minimization).  The primal problem is to maximize a linear objective function subject to linear constraints (AX ≤ b and x ≥ 0). The dual problem is to minimize a different linear objective function subject to different constraints (A<sup>T</sup>y ≥ c and y ≥ 0).  The slide highlights the relationship between the two problems using arrows to visually connect them.  The label "Dual" is circled, indicating that the bottom problem is the dual of the top problem.  This is a foundational slide for understanding the duality theorem in linear programming.  The variables $x$ and $y$ represent vectors of decision variables, $c$ is a vector of coefficients for the objective function in the primal problem, and $b$ is a vector of coefficients for the objective function in the dual problem.  $A$ is the constraint matrix.  The notation $c^T x$ and $b^T y$ represent the dot products of the vectors.  The inequality signs (≤ and ≥) are crucial to understanding the relationship between the primal and dual problems.

$a_{ij}$ = amount of $N_i$ in 1 unit of $F_j$

$c_j$ = price (cost) of $F_j$
$x_j$ = amount of $F_j$ consumption

\textbf{Description:}

This slide defines the variables and parameters for a diet problem, a specific type of linear programming problem.  It's likely part of a larger lecture on linear programming, and this slide is setting up the context for a specific application.  The variables represent the amount of each food to consume to meet the minimum nutritional requirements at the lowest cost.  $m$ represents the number of nutrients, $n$ represents the number of foods, $a_{ij}$ is the amount of nutrient $i$ in food $j$, $b_i$ is the minimum monthly intake of nutrient $i$, and $c_j$ is the price of food $j$.  $x_j$ represents the amount of food $j$ to consume.  The problem is to find the optimal combination of foods to meet the minimum nutritional requirements at the lowest possible cost.  This is a common example used to illustrate linear programming concepts.

\textbf{Diet Problem}

Minimize cost: $Z(x) = \sum_{j} c_j x_j$

s.t. $\sum_{j} a_{ij} x_j \ge b_i$
$x_j \ge 0$

\textbf{Description:}

This slide presents the mathematical formulation of the diet problem as a linear programming problem.  It shows the objective function, which is to minimize the total cost of the food consumed, represented by $Z(x) = \sum_{j} c_j x_j$.  The constraints are shown as $\sum_{j} a_{ij} x_j \ge b_i$, ensuring that the minimum intake of each nutrient ($b_i$) is met.  The non-negativity constraint $x_j \ge 0$ ensures that the amount of each food consumed is non-negative.  This is a standard linear programming problem format, where the objective is to minimize a linear function subject to linear constraints.  The variables $x_j$ represent the amount of food $j$ to consume, $c_j$ represents the cost of food $j$, $a_{ij}$ represents the amount of nutrient $i$ in food $j$, and $b_i$ represents the minimum intake of nutrient $i$.  The notation $\sum_{j}$ indicates summation over all foods.  The slide is likely part of a larger lecture on linear programming and its applications, specifically focusing on the diet problem.

Slide 7

Replacement of food by pills for each $N_i$'s
$N_i$ = pills  $i=1,2,\dots,m$
$y_i$ = unit price of pill for $N_i$
1 unit of $F_j$ contains $a_{ij}$ unit of $N_i$
Price/Value of (1 unit) $F_j = \sum_{i} y_i a_{ij}$
Maximize profit by selling pills

\textbf{Description:}

This slide describes a variation of the diet problem, where instead of minimizing the cost of food, the objective is to maximize the profit from selling pills that contain nutrients.  The $N_i$ represent nutrients, and $F_j$ represent foods.  The $a_{ij}$ values are the same as in the previous diet problem slides, representing the amount of nutrient $i$ in food $j$.  The $y_i$ values represent the unit price of a pill containing nutrient $i$.  The key change is that the value of each food item ($F_j$) is now calculated as the sum of the values of the nutrients it contains, weighted by the unit price of each nutrient pill ($y_i$).  This formulation allows for a different optimization problem, where the goal is to maximize the profit from selling nutrient pills derived from the foods.  This is a transformation of the original diet problem to a profit maximization problem.


Slide 8

\textbf{Diet Problem - Dual Formulation}

Maximize (profit) $Z(y) = \sum_{i} b_i y_i$

s.t. $\sum_{i} y_i a_{ij} \le c_j$
$y_i \ge 0$

\textbf{Description:}

This slide presents the dual formulation of the diet problem, derived from the previous slide's discussion of replacing food with nutrient pills.  The objective function is to maximize the profit ($Z(y)$) by selling nutrient pills, where the profit is the sum of the minimum intake of each nutrient ($b_i$) multiplied by the unit price of each nutrient pill ($y_i$).  The constraints ensure that the price/value of each food item ($F_j$) is not greater than the cost of the food item ($c_j$).  The non-negativity constraint $y_i \ge 0$ ensures that the unit price of each nutrient pill is non-negative.  This dual formulation is a crucial step in understanding the duality theorem in linear programming, showing the relationship between the primal (minimizing cost) and dual (maximizing profit) problems.  The $a_{ij}$ values represent the amount of nutrient $i$ in food $j$, $b_i$ represents the minimum intake of nutrient $i$, and $c_j$ represents the cost of food $j$.  The slide is likely part of a larger lecture on linear programming and its applications, specifically focusing on the diet problem and duality.
