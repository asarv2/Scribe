Slide 8

\textbf{Primal Network Simplex}

\textit{With a little thought, one realizes that the selection rule for the leaving arc in a primal pivot is as follows:}

\textit{Leaving arc selection rule:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item the leaving arc must be oriented along the cycle in the reverse direction from the entering arc, and
    \item among all such arcs, it must have the smallest flow.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Also, the flows on the cycle get updated as follows:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Flows oriented in the same direction as the leaving arc are decreased by the amount of flow that was on the leaving arc, whereas flows in the opposite direction are increased by this amount.
\end{itemize}


\textbf{Description:}

This slide provides further details on the Network Simplex Method, specifically the rule for selecting the leaving arc during a primal pivot step.  It explains that the leaving arc must be oriented in the opposite direction of the entering arc within the cycle.  Crucially, the leaving arc must have the smallest flow among all arcs in the cycle that satisfy this orientation requirement.  The slide also details how the flows on the cycle are updated.  Flows in the same direction as the leaving arc are decreased by the amount of flow on the leaving arc, while flows in the opposite direction are increased by the same amount.  This ensures that the spanning tree remains valid and the flow values are adjusted correctly during the optimization process.

\textbf{Dual Network Simplex}

To this end, let us consider the general situation. As mentioned above, the spanning tree with the leaving arc removed consists of two disjoint trees. The entering arc must reconnect these two trees.

First, consider a reconnecting arc that connects in the same direction as the leaving arc. If chosen as the entering arc, this arc's slack will drop from a positive value to zero. The dual slack for all other nontree arcs pointing in the same direction will drop by the same amount. The leaving arc will become a nontree arc and therefore its dual slack will also drop by this amount. But, it started out at zero. After the pivot, it will be negative. In other words, dual feasibility will be lost. For this reason, we restrict our attention to reconnecting arcs that point in the opposite direction.

\textit{Entering arc selection rule:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item the entering arc must bridge the two subtrees in the opposite direction from the leaving arc, and
    \item among all such arcs, it must have the smallest dual slack.
\end{itemize}


\textbf{Description:}

This slide describes the rule for selecting the entering arc in the dual network simplex method when the spanning tree has been disconnected by the removal of a leaving arc.  The key idea is that if a reconnecting arc is chosen that points in the same direction as the leaving arc, the dual feasibility of the solution will be lost.  Therefore, the algorithm focuses on reconnecting arcs that point in the opposite direction.  The selection rule for the entering arc is based on finding the arc that bridges the two subtrees in the opposite direction from the leaving arc and has the smallest dual slack among all such arcs.  This ensures that the dual feasibility is maintained during the pivot step.

\textbf{5. Putting It All Together}

As we saw in Chapter 5, for linear programming the primal and the dual simplex methods form the foundation on which one can build a few different variants of the simplex method. The same is true here in the context of network flows.

For example, one can build a two-phased network simplex method (with costs artificially and temporarily altered to ensure dual feasibility of an initial tree solution) to find a primal feasible solution and then uses the primal network simplex method to move from the feasible solution to an optimal one.

Alternatively, one can use the primal network simplex method (with supplies temporarily altered to ensure primal feasibility of an initial tree solution) to find a dual feasible solution and then use the dual network simplex method (with the original supplies) to move from the dual feasible solution to an optimal one.

Finally, as described for linear programming in Chapter 7, one can define a parametric self-dual method in which primal pivots and dual pivots are intertwined as needed so as to reduce a perturbation parameter $\mu$ from $\infty$ to zero.
