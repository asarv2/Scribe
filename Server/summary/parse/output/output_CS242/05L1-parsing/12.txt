# Slide 12

**Order of operations**

There is an order of operations in regular expressions

*   `abc|def` matches the strings "abc" or "def", not "ab(c or d)ef"
*   You can get around this using parenthesis e.g., a(bc|de)f

By default, regular expressions try to capture as much text as possible (greedy matching)

*   `<(.*)>` applied to `<tag>text</tag>` will match the entire expression
*   If you want to capture the least amount of text possible use `<(.*?)>`
the ? makes the matching lazy rather than greedy will just match the `<a>` term


---

**Description:**

This slide, part of the Intro to Data Science course, discusses the order of operations in regular expressions (regex) and how to control the matching behavior.

*   **Order of Operations:** The first bullet point explains that regex operators have a specific order of execution.  The example `abc|def` illustrates that the `|` (OR) operator has lower precedence than the other operators.  This means that `abc|def` will match either "abc" or "def", but not "ab(c or d)ef".

*   **Parentheses for Grouping:** The second bullet point explains how parentheses can be used to change the order of operations and create capturing groups.  The example `a(bc|de)f` shows how parentheses force the `|` operator to apply to the `bc` or `de` parts, allowing the regex to match "abcf" or "adef".

*   **Greedy Matching:** The next section explains that by default, regex engines are "greedy," meaning they try to match as much text as possible.  The example `<(.*)>` applied to `<tag>text</tag>` illustrates this.  The `(.*)` part will match the entire string between the `<tag>` and `</tag>` tags, not just the first `<tag>`.

*   **Lazy Matching:** The final bullet point introduces the concept of "lazy" matching.  The example `<(.*?)>` shows how to use the `?` quantifier to make the matching lazy, meaning it will match the *least* amount of text possible.  The example `<(.*?)>` applied to `<tag>text</tag>` will only match the `<a>` part.

The slide is crucial for understanding how to control the behavior of regex matches, especially when dealing with complex patterns in text data.  The examples are clear and illustrate the differences between greedy and lazy matching.
