**plt.subplots**

`plt.subplots(nrows=1, ncols=1, sharex=False, sharey=False, ...)`

*   `nrows`, `ncols` define a grid of subplots and axes
*   `sharex`, `sharey` will use the same x and y limits on the axes
*   Returns the figure and a list of the axes (or single axes in the 1x1 case)

*   **nrows, ncols:** These parameters define the number of rows and columns in the grid of subplots.  This is crucial for creating multiple plots within a single figure.
*   **sharex, sharey:** These parameters control whether the x-axis and y-axis limits are shared across the subplots. This is important for maintaining consistency or allowing for different scales in different subplots.
*   **Return Value:** The function returns the figure and a list of the axes.  This allows for further manipulation and customization of the individual subplots.

```python
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
%matplotlib inline
X = data[['x1','x2','x3','x4']]
Y = data[['y1','y2','y3','y4']]
for i in range(0,4):
    fig, ax = plt.subplots()
    ax.scatter(X.iloc[:,i],Y.iloc[:,i], marker = mark[i], color=colours[i])
    ax.set_title("Set "+str(i+1))
    ax.set_xlim(0,20)
    ax.set_ylim(0,15)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(nrows=2, ncols=2, sharex=True, sharey=True)
for i in range(0,4):
    sp = ax[i//2, i%2]
    sp.scatter(X.iloc[:,i],Y.iloc[:,i], marker = mark[i], color=colours[i])
    sp.set_title("Set "+str(i+1))
fig.tight_layout()
```

Two parameters:

**Basic Principles of Visualization**

*   **Data Integrity:**  Ensure the data you're visualizing accurately reflects the underlying information.  Avoid misleading or distorted representations.

*   **Clear and Concise:**  Use clear labels, titles, and legends to make the visualization easy to understand.  Avoid clutter and unnecessary details.

*   **Appropriate Choice of Visualization:** Select the most suitable visualization type for the data and the message you want to convey.  Consider the nature of the data (categorical, continuous, relationships) and the insights you're trying to extract.

*   **Context and Story:**  Provide context for the visualization.  Explain the source of the data, the variables being displayed, and the relationships being highlighted.  Frame the visualization within a narrative or story to make the insights more impactful.

*   **Accessibility and Inclusivity:**  Design visualizations that are accessible to a wide audience.  Use clear and contrasting colors, appropriate font sizes, and avoid relying solely on color to convey information.  Consider the needs of individuals with visual impairments.

*   **Interactivity and Exploration:**  Where appropriate, use interactive elements to allow users to explore the data further.  This can include zooming, filtering, and highlighting specific data points.

*   **Visual Appeal and Aesthetics:**  While not the primary goal, make the visualization visually appealing and aesthetically pleasing.  Use appropriate colors, fonts, and layouts to enhance readability and engagement.

# Slide 60

**Choosing a Good Visualization**

*   **What are you trying to show?**  Consider the specific message or insight you want to communicate.  Are you looking to show a relationship, a distribution, a comparison, or the composition of something?

*   **What kind of data do you have?**  The type of data (categorical, numerical, time-series, etc.) will influence the best visualization method.

*   **What is the best way to show the data?**  Consider different visualization types (bar charts, scatter plots, histograms, line graphs, etc.) and how they can effectively represent the data and the message you want to convey.

*   **How can you make the graph easy to understand?**  Use clear labels, titles, and legends.  Ensure the graph is well-organized and visually appealing.  Consider the target audience and how they will interpret the information.
