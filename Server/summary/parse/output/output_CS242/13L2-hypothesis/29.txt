**Two-sided vs. one-sided tests**

The previous code considered deviation from the null hypothesis in both directions (two-sides test is default)
This corresponds to H₀ = 30, H₁ ≠ 30
We actually wanted to compute a one-sided test: H₀ = 30, H₁ > 30
In this case, the probability corresponds to the area under the *left* side of the curve.

**Figure 1:**

A bell-shaped curve, likely a normal distribution, is plotted in the upper-right quadrant of the slide. The x-axis is labeled "x̄" and ranges from approximately -3 to 3. The y-axis is labeled "p(x̄ | H₀)" and ranges from 0 to 0.4.  The curve is centered around 0 on the x-axis.  A shaded area is visible under the left tail of the curve, extending from approximately -3 to a value slightly to the left of -2.  This shaded area represents the probability of observing the data (or more extreme data) if the null hypothesis is true.  The figure is used to illustrate the difference between a two-sided and one-sided test.  The shaded area under the left tail of the curve is the p-value for a one-sided test, whereas the area under both tails would be the p-value for a two-sided test.
