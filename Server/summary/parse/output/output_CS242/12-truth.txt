**Bugs in data interpretation**

If visualizations are an accurate and true model of our data, why do we make mistakes interpreting patterns from our plots?

* **Patternicity bug:** Eyes detect interesting patterns regardless, real or not
* **Storytelling bug:** Coherent explanations for patterns are compelling, real or not
* **Confirmation bug:** Evidence confirming our position is valued over that which refutes it

# Slide 22

**Arthritis study (Redelmeier & Tversky '96)**

Common belief: Arthritis pain is associated with changes in weather

Experiment: Followed 18 arthritis patients for 15 months; 2 x per month assessed: (1) pain and joint tenderness, and (2) weather

Results: No correlation between pain/tenderness and weather

Patients saw correlation that did not exist... why?

* Patients noticed when bad weather and pain co-occurred, but failed to notice when they didn't.

Confirmation bias: People often seek information that confirms rather than disconfirms their original hypothesis


---

**Description of what you see:**

This slide presents a case study, likely from an Introduction to Data Science course, illustrating the concept of confirmation bias.  The study, conducted by Redelmeier and Tversky, investigated the perceived relationship between arthritis pain and weather.  The text describes a common belief that arthritis pain is linked to weather changes.  The experiment followed 18 arthritis patients for 15 months, recording pain/tenderness and weather conditions twice per month.  The results, however, showed no correlation between the two.  Crucially, the slide highlights that patients were more likely to notice instances where bad weather and pain coincided, but failed to register instances where there was no such coincidence.  This observation directly demonstrates confirmation bias, where individuals tend to favor information that supports their pre-existing beliefs and overlook contradictory evidence.  The slide is designed to illustrate how cognitive biases can influence data interpretation and lead to inaccurate conclusions.

# Slide 24

**Gambler's fallacy**

Gambler's fallacy: belief that if deviations from expected behavior are observed in repeated independent trials, then future deviations in the opposite direction are then more likely

T&K: this is an example of the representativeness heuristic—where the probability of an event is judged by its similarity to the population from which sample is drawn

The sequence "H T H T T H" is seen as more representative of a prototypical coin sequence. Why?

* When people are asked to make up random sequences, they tend to make the proportion of H and T closer to 50% than would be expected by random chance
* T&K interpretation: people believe that short sequences should be representative of longer ones


---

**Description of what you see:**

This slide, part of an Introduction to Data Science lecture, discusses the gambler's fallacy and the representativeness heuristic.  The text explains the gambler's fallacy as the belief that deviations from expected behavior in repeated trials will be followed by deviations in the opposite direction.  It connects this to the representativeness heuristic, where the probability of an event is judged by its similarity to the population from which the sample is drawn.
