# Slide 23

**Estimating probabilities (Tversky & Kahneman '73/'74)**

Question: Which causes more deaths in developed countries?
(a) traffic accidents or (b) stomach cancer
Typical guess: traffic accident = 4X stomach cancer
Actual: 45,000 traffic, 95,000 stomach cancer deaths in US
Ratio of newspaper reports on each subject: 137 (traffic fatality) to 1 (stomach cancer death)

Availability heuristic: Tendency for people to make judgments of frequency on basis of how easily examples come to mind

**Description of what you see:**

This slide, part of an Introduction to Data Science lecture, presents an example illustrating the availability heuristic.  The example uses the question of which causes more deaths in developed countries: traffic accidents or stomach cancer.  The typical guess is that traffic accidents cause four times more deaths than stomach cancer.  However, the actual figures show that stomach cancer causes significantly more deaths (95,000) than traffic accidents (45,000) in the US.  The ratio of newspaper reports further highlights the bias: there are 137 times more reports about traffic fatalities than stomach cancer deaths.  The slide is designed to illustrate how readily available information (in this case, media reports) can influence our judgments of frequency and probability, even when the actual data suggests otherwise.  The example is a classic demonstration of the availability heuristic, a cognitive bias that affects our estimations of probabilities.
