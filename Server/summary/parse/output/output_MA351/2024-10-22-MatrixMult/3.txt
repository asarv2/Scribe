\section*{Composition of Linear Transformations}

$R^l \xrightarrow{B_{mxl}} R^m \xrightarrow{A_{nxm}} R^n$
$X \xrightarrow{} Y=BX \xrightarrow{} Z=AY = A(BX) = (AB)X$

$Z = AY = A( \chi_1 B_1 + \chi_2 B_2 + \cdots + \chi_l B_l) = \chi_1 A B_1 + \chi_2 A B_2 + \cdots + \chi_l A B_l = \begin{bmatrix} A B_1 & A B_2 & \cdots & A B_l \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \chi_1 \\ \chi_2 \\ \vdots \\ \chi_l \end{bmatrix}$

\textbf{Description:}

This slide builds upon the previous slides by illustrating the composition of linear transformations using matrices.  It shows how applying a transformation $B$ followed by a transformation $A$ is equivalent to a single transformation $AB$.  The slide visually represents this composition with arrows indicating the flow of vectors through the transformations.  The key takeaway is that the composition of linear transformations represented by matrices $A$ and $B$ results in a new linear transformation represented by the matrix product $AB$.  The notation and the context of the slide strongly suggest this is part of a linear algebra lecture, likely leading into a discussion of linear programming, where matrix operations are crucial for defining and solving problems.  The slide explicitly shows how the resulting transformation $Z$ is a linear combination of the transformed basis vectors.
