Slide 4

\textbf{In-pretation of Diagonalization} : $A = QD Q^{-1}$

(Change of Basis)

Let $b \in \mathbb{R}^n$.

Write $b = c_1 X_1 + c_2 X_2 + \dots + c_n X_n$

Then $Ab = A(c_1 X_1 + c_2 X_2 + \dots + c_n X_n)$

$= c_1 A X_1 + c_2 A X_2 + \dots + c_n A X_n$

$= c_1 \lambda_1 X_1 + c_2 \lambda_2 X_2 + \dots + c_n \lambda_n X_n$

$b = [X_1 \quad X_2 \quad \dots \quad X_n] \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \\ c_n \end{bmatrix} = Qc$

$Ab = Q D Q^{-1} Q c = Q D c$

$b = Qc \implies c = Q^{-1} b$

$Ab = Q D c = Q D Q^{-1} b$

$b = Qc \implies c = Q^{-1} b$

\textbf{Description:}

This slide explains the interpretation of diagonalization in terms of a change of basis.  It shows how applying a linear transformation (represented by matrix $A$) to a vector $b$ in the original basis can be equivalent to applying a diagonal transformation (represented by matrix $D$) to the coordinates of $b$ in a new basis formed by the eigenvectors of $A$.  The slide introduces the concept of changing the basis from the standard basis to a basis formed by the eigenvectors of $A$.  The vector $b$ is expressed in terms of the eigenvectors $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ with coefficients $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_n$.  The transformation $A$ acts on $b$ and the result is expressed in terms of the eigenvectors and their corresponding eigenvalues.  The slide also shows the relationship between the coordinates in the original basis ($b$) and the coordinates in the new basis ($c$).  The formula $c = Q^{-1} b$ shows how to find the coordinates in the new basis given the coordinates in the original basis.  The notation is consistent with linear algebra, using matrices and vectors.  The slide likely follows a lecture on linear algebra, where diagonalization is a key concept.
