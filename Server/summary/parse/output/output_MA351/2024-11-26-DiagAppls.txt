\textbf{Diagonalization of a matrix}

Given $A^{nxn}$ (assume diagonalizable, $g_i = m_i$ for all)

$A x_i = \lambda_i x_i$, $i=1,2,\dots,n$

$[AX_1 \quad AX_2 \quad \dots \quad AX_n] = [\lambda_1 x_1 \quad \lambda_2 x_2 \quad \dots \quad \lambda_n x_n]$

$A[x_1 \quad \dots \quad x_n] = [\lambda_1 x_1 \quad \dots \quad \lambda_n x_n]$

$A = QDQ^{-1}$

\textbf{Ex 1}

$A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$,

$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = 1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 2 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

\textbf{Ex 2}

$A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ 3 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$,

$\begin{bmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ 3 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = 2 \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$

$\begin{bmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ 3 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = 3 \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$

The slide also shows the matrix multiplications involved in verifying the diagonalization.  The notation and formatting are typical of lecture notes on linear algebra. Slide 4

\textbf{In-pretation of Diagonalization} : $A = QD Q^{-1}$

(Change of Basis)

Let $b \in \mathbb{R}^n$.

Write $b = c_1 X_1 + c_2 X_2 + \dots + c_n X_n$

Then $Ab = A(c_1 X_1 + c_2 X_2 + \dots + c_n X_n)$

$= c_1 A X_1 + c_2 A X_2 + \dots + c_n A X_n$

$= c_1 \lambda_1 X_1 + c_2 \lambda_2 X_2 + \dots + c_n \lambda_n X_n$

$b = [X_1 \quad X_2 \quad \dots \quad X_n] \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \\ c_n \end{bmatrix} = Qc$

$Ab = Q D Q^{-1} Q c = Q D c$

$b = Qc \implies c = Q^{-1} b$

$Ab = Q D c = Q D Q^{-1} b$

$b = Qc \implies c = Q^{-1} b$

\textbf{Description:}

This slide explains the interpretation of diagonalization in terms of a change of basis.  It shows how applying a linear transformation (represented by matrix $A$) to a vector $b$ in the original basis can be equivalent to applying a diagonal transformation (represented by matrix $D$) to the coordinates of $b$ in a new basis formed by the eigenvectors of $A$.  The slide introduces the concept of changing the basis from the standard basis to a basis formed by the eigenvectors of $A$.  The vector $b$ is expressed in terms of the eigenvectors $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ with coefficients $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_n$.  The transformation $A$ acts on $b$ and the result is expressed in terms of the eigenvectors and their corresponding eigenvalues.  The slide also shows the relationship between the coordinates in the original basis ($b$) and the coordinates in the new basis ($c$).  The formula $c = Q^{-1} b$ shows how to find the coordinates in the new basis given the coordinates in the original basis.  The notation is consistent with linear algebra, using matrices and vectors.  The slide likely follows a lecture on linear algebra, where diagonalization is a key concept.

Slide 6

Application of Diagonalization

Computation of $A^k$

$A = QD Q^{-1}$

$A^2 = QD^2 Q^{-1}$

$A^3 = QD^3 Q^{-1}$

$A^k = QD^k Q^{-1}$

$D^k = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1^k & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2^k & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & \lambda_n^k \end{bmatrix}$

$A^k b = Q D^k Q^{-1} b = Q \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1^k & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2^k & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & \lambda_n^k \end{bmatrix} Q^{-1} b = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & x_2 & \dots & x_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1^k \\ \lambda_2^k \\ \vdots \\ \lambda_n^k \end{bmatrix}$

\textbf{Description:}

This slide demonstrates how diagonalization can be used to efficiently compute powers of a matrix $A$, denoted as $A^k$.  It shows that if a matrix $A$ can be diagonalized as $A = QD Q^{-1}$, then the $k$th power of $A$ is given by $A^k = QD^k Q^{-1}$, where $D$ is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues of $A$.  The slide also shows how to compute $D^k$, which is simply raising each diagonal element of $D$ to the power of $k$.  This method is significantly more efficient than repeatedly multiplying the matrix by itself.  The notation is consistent with linear algebra, using matrices and vectors.  The slide likely follows a lecture on linear algebra, where diagonalization is a key concept.

$b_n =$ population in $B$ in year $n$

Let $a_0 = 38(k)$, $b_0 = 62(k)$

$a_1 = 0.9 a_0 + 0.07 b_0$

$b_1 = 0.1 a_0 + 0.93 b_0$

Slide 8

Let $P_0 = \begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ b_0 \end{bmatrix}$, $P_1 = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ b_1 \end{bmatrix}, \dots, P_n = \begin{bmatrix} a_n \\ b_n \end{bmatrix}$

Then $P_1 = A P_0$, $P_2 = A P_1 = A^2 P_0$, $P_3 = A P_2 = A^3 P_0$, $\dots$

$P_n = A^n P_0$

$Q: \lim_{n \to \infty} P_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} A^n P_0 = ?$

Ans: Find $\lambda, X$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.07 \\ 0.1 & 0.93 \end{bmatrix}$

$\det(A - \lambda I) = \det \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 - \lambda & 0.07 \\ 0.1 & 0.93 - \lambda \end{bmatrix}$

$= (0.9 - \lambda)(0.93 - \lambda) - 0.007$

$= \lambda^2 - 1.83 \lambda + 0.83$

$= (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 0.83)$

$\lambda_1 = 0.83$


\textbf{Description:}

This slide continues the Markov chain analysis from the previous slide.  It shows the recursive relationship between the population vectors $P_n$ and the transition matrix $A$.  The key concept is finding the limiting population distribution as $n$ approaches infinity.  The slide shows the calculation of the eigenvalues of the transition matrix $A$ to determine the long-term behavior of the Markov chain.  The calculation of the determinant of $(A - \lambda I)$ is shown to find the eigenvalues $\lambda$.  The result, $\lambda_1 = 0.83$, is a crucial step in determining the long-term population distribution.  The notation is consistent with linear algebra, using matrices and vectors.
