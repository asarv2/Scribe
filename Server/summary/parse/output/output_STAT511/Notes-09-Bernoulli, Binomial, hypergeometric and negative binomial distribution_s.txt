**Discrete Distribution I: Bernoulli and Binomial distributions**

Suppose a pmf function is completely determined by some quantity. Such a quantity is called a parameter of the distribution. The collection of all probability distributions for different values of the parameter is called a family of probability distributions. In the section, we study some popular discrete distribution families.

**(1) Bernoulli trial:** A Bernoulli trail is an experiment with two and only two possible outcomes. And a random variable X has a Bernoulli(p) distribution if its pmf follows:

P(X = 1) = p,
P(X = 0) = 1 − p

where 0 and 1 stand for two different outcomes (usually called failure and success). X is the indicator function of a success.

X = {0,   failure
     1,   success}

The mean and variance of a Bernoulli(p) random variable are easily seen to be

E(X) = 1 × p + 0 × (1 − p) = p
Var(X) = (1 − p)² × p + (0 − p)² × (1 − p) = p(1 − p)

Consider a sequence of Bernoulli random variables. If there are n independent and identical Bernoulli random variables, the summation of them gives a binomial random variable.

**(2) Binomial experiment:** An experiment that satisfies the following conditions is called a Binomial experiment.

1. The experiment consists of a sequence of n Bernoulli trials, where n is fixed in advance of the experiment.

2. Each trial can result in one of the same two possible outcomes (dichotomous trials), which we generically denote by success (S) and failure (F).

3. The trials are independent, so that the outcome on any particular trial does not influence the outcome on any other trial.

4. The probability of success P(S) is constant from trial to trial; we denote this probability by p.
In most Binomial experiments, it is the total number of S’s, rather than knowledge of exactly which trials yielded S’s, that is of interest.

Definition: The Binomial random variable X associated with a binomial experiment consisting of n trials is defined as

X = the number of S’s among the n trials.

Hypergeometric and Negative Binomial 

The hypergeometric and negative binomial distributions are both related to the binomial distribution. 
Suppose there are N balls (M red balls, and N-M black balls) in a urn. Every time, you draw a ball from 
the urn. Let X be the total number of red balls among n draws. 
• 
If every time after you draw the ball, you actually put the ball back into the urn, i.e. draw with 
replacement, then X follows a 𝐵𝑖𝑛(𝑛,𝑀/𝑁) distribution. 
• 
If every time after you draw the ball, you don’t put the ball back into the urn, i.e. draw without 
replacement, then X follows the so-called hypergeometric distribution ℎ𝑚(𝑛, 𝑀, 𝑁). 

The hypergeometric distribution is the exact probability model for the number of S’s when sampling 
without replacement from a finite dichotomous (S or F) population, where the sample size is n and the 
population size N. 

(3) The hypergeometric distribution 
1. The population or set to be sampled consists of N individuals, objects, or elements (a finite 
population). 

2. Each individual can be characterized as a success (S) or a failure (F), and there are M 
successes in the population. 

3. A sample of n individuals is selected without replacement in such a way that each subset of size 
n is equally likely to be chosen. 
The random variable of interest is X = the number of S’s in the sample. The probability distribution of X 
depends on the parameters n, M, and N.

**Discrete Distribution III: Negative Binomial and Geometric Distributions**

The experiment follows the same setting of the binomial experiment, except that the experiment continues (trials are performed) until a total of *r* successes have been observed, where *r* is a specified positive integer. Let *X* be the number of failures that precede the *r*<sup>th</sup> success, then *X* has a Negative Binomial distribution, denoted as *NBin(r, p)*.

**pmf of *NBin(r, p):**

*nb(x; r, p) =  (x + r − 1 choose r − 1) p<sup>r</sup>(1 − p)<sup>x</sup>,  x = 0, 1, ...*

**Note:** The last trial must be a success (S) and there must be (r − 1) successes among the first x + r − 1 trials. Consider *nb(7; 3, p) = P(X = 7)*, the probability that exactly 7 failures (F's) occur before the 3<sup>rd</sup> success (S).

**Mean and variance of *NBin(r, p):**

* E(X) = r(1 − p)/p
* Var(X) = r(1 − p)/p<sup>2</sup>

**Example 9.6:** A pediatrician wishes to recruit 5 couples, each of whom is expecting their first child, to participate in a new natural childbirth regimen. Let *p* = *P*(a randomly selected couple agrees to participate). If *p* = 0.2, what is the probability that 15 couples must be asked before 5 are found who agree to participate? Let *S* = {agrees to participate}. This is about the probability that 10 F's occur before the fifth S.  *P(X = 10) = nb(10; 5, 0.2) = ...*

The probability that at most 10 F's are observed (at most 10 + 5 couples are asked) is *P(X ≤ 10) = ...*

**Geometric distribution:** In the special case *r* = 1, the pmf of the negative binomial is geometric distribution.

*nb(x; 1, p) = (1 − p)<sup>x</sup>p,  x = 0, 1, 2...*

**Example 9.7:** Let *X* now be the number of children born up to and including the first boy. Let *Y* be the number of girls born up to the first boy, then *Y ~ nbin(y; 1, p)*.  *X = Y + 1 or Y = X − 1*.

*P<sub>X</sub>(x) = P<sub>X</sub>(X = x) = P<sub>Y</sub>(Y = x − 1) = p(1 − p)<sup>x−1</sup> for x = 1, 2, 3...*

*E(X) = 1/p or E(X) = E(Y + 1) = E(Y) + 1 = 1/p*

*Var(X) = (1 − p)/p<sup>2</sup> or Var(X) = Var(Y + 1) = Var(Y) = (1 − p)/p<sup>2</sup>*
