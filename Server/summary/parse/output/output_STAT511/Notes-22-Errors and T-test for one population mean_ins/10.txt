**Hypothesis tests about the one-sample population mean 8.2-8.3 (continued)**

**Example 22.6:** Biologists studying the healing of skin wounds measured the rate at which new cells closed a razor cut made in the skin of an anesthetized newt. Here are data from *n* = 18 newts (assume randomly selected), measured in micrometers (millionths of a meter) per hour (μm/h):

29  27  34  40  22  28  14  35  26
35  12  30  23  18  11  22  23  33

1. We are interested in 5% level hypothesis testing the average healing rate:

   H<sub>0</sub>: μ = 30 vs H<sub>1</sub>: μ ≠ 30

2. *n* < 30 but the histogram and normal quantile plot show the data is roughly normal and the boxplot shows there are no outliers. Therefore, we can assume the population distribution is normal.

   t<sub>TS</sub> = (X̄ - 30) / (s/√18) = -2.21.

3. t critical value is 2.1098.

4. |t<sub>TS</sub>| > 2.1098, we reject the null hypothesis.  There is statistical evidence that the mean healing rate is not 30 at the 0.05 level.


**Mean:** 25.67
**Std Dev:** 8.324
**Median:** 26.5


**Sample size determination (optional):**

Consider one-sided test H<sub>a</sub>: μ > μ<sub>0</sub>, then

β(μ′) = P(X̄ - μ<sub>0</sub> / (s/√n) < t<sub>α,n-1</sub>) = P(N(√n(μ′ - μ<sub>0</sub>)/σ, 1) < t<sub>α,n-1</sub>)

Intuitively, if |μ′ - μ|/σ increases, β decreases.

But, the exact calculation is not straightforward, and involves the non-central t distribution. As a consequence, the sample size determination doesn't have a close formula.


**R code:**

power.t.test(delta = 1, sd = 2.5, sig.level = 0.05, power = 0.8, type = "one.sample", alternative = "two.sided")
power.t.test(n = 12, delta = 1, sd = 2.5, sig.level = 0.05, type = "one.sample", alternative = "two.sided")
