**Sample size calculation for a very simple case**

* The location of a CI for μ is determined by the point estimator x̄.
* The accuracy is determined by the width of the CI, which depends on:
    * the confidence level C.
    * the original data variability σ.
    * the sample size n.

| Confidence level C | 90% | 95% | 99% |
|---|---|---|---|
| Critical value z* | 1.645 | 1.960 | 2.576 |

* If we want to control the width to be smaller than a fixed value w, (that is margin of error is smaller than w/2), the necessary sample size is at least (2 * z* * σ / w)<sup>2</sup>
The margin of error = z* * σ / √n < w/2

(z* * σ / (w/2))<sup>2</sup> < n
n = ⌈(z* * σ / (w/2))<sup>2</sup>⌉

* If we want to estimate the proportion p within a specified margin of error m, the required sample size is at least (2 * z*/w)<sup>2</sup> * p*(1 - p*). (HW8-#8.b)
    * p* is called the guessed value based on previous knowledge.
    * If we have no information, we may replace p* above with 0.5 to obtain the most conservative sample size. If p* isn’t given in the problem, use 0.5. Why?

Inequality: |a - b| ≤ ((a + b) / 2)<sup>2</sup> with equality iff a = b.

p(1 - p) ≤ [(p + (1 - p)) / 2]<sup>2</sup> = 1/4 with equality iff p = 1 - p <=> p = 0.5

I. Large sample CI for population mean μ

Our CI formula in last section requires: 1) normality, 2) known standard deviation. CI for the population mean μ is,

(𝑥̅ − z∗∙σ/√n, 𝑥̅ + z∗∙σ/√n)

Now we consider if these conditions don’t hold. Suppose X<sub>1</sub>, …, X<sub>n</sub> are random sample with unknown mean μ and unknown variance σ<sup>2</sup>.

First of all, even they are not normal rv’s, by CLT, we still have the large sample approximation that

𝑥̅ ≈ N(μ, σ<sup>2</sup>/n) and  (𝑥̅−μ)/(σ/√n) ≈ Z

If furthermore, if we have a good estimation σ̂ for σ, we can plug in this estimator, then we obtain

(𝑥̅−μ)/(σ̂/√n) ≈ Z

One popular estimator for population standard deviation is sample standard deviation. Thus, it leads to the following large sample approximated confident interval with C confidence level:

(𝑥̅ − z∗∙S/√n, 𝑥̅ + z∗∙S/√n)


Note: Because S = √(1/(n-1) * Σ(X<sub>i</sub> − 𝑥̅)<sup>2</sup>), the margin of error here is a not fixed value, but data-dependent.

III. Small sample CI for population mean under normal distribution and unknown 𝜎<sup>2</sup>

Suppose 𝑋<sub>1</sub>, …, 𝑋<sub>𝑛</sub> are random samples with unknown mean 𝜇 and unknown variance 𝜎<sup>2</sup>.

As we studied, 
(𝑥̅ − μ) / (S/√n) ≈ Z holds and the CI for μ presented in earlier sections is valid when the sample size is sufficiently large. The resulting interval can be used whatever the nature of the population distribution.

The CLT cannot be invoked, however, when n is small. Statisticians derive the exact distribution of (𝑥̅ − μ) / (S/√n) when all 𝑋’s are normal. In this section, we will focus on a CI for a normal population distribution.

Assumption: the population of interest is normal, so that 𝑋<sub>1</sub>, …, 𝑋<sub>𝑛</sub> constitutes a random sample from a normal distribution with both 𝜇 and 𝜎 unknown.

The result on which inferences are based introduces a new family of probability distributions called t distributions.

Theorem: When 𝑥̅ is the mean of a random sample of size n from a normal distribution with mean 𝜇, the rv

𝑇 = (𝑥̅ − 𝜇) / (𝑆/√𝑛)

has a probability distribution called a student-t distribution with n – 1 degrees of freedom (df).

We know that a normal distribution is governed by two parameters; each different choice of 𝜇 in combination with 𝜎 gives a particular normal distribution. Any particular t distribution results from specifying the value of a single parameter, called the number of degrees of freedom, abbreviated df.

Proposition of t distribution: Let 𝑡<sub>𝑣</sub> denote the t distribution with 𝑣 df.

* It has a bell shape and centers at 0.
* It is more spread out than normal.
* The df controls the variability of t distribution.

Comparison between Z and T CIs

Intuitively, narrower CI means more precise interval estimation. For normal means estimation, if population standard deviation is known, then both z- and t- confidence interval are statistically valid CI.

Question: Can we compute both of them and pick the narrow one?

Comparison between Z and t confidence intervals: the same interval center, the different interval width.

CI vs. PI

Prediction Interval for a new observation from this normal distribution: Let X<sub>n+1</sub> be a new independent observation. We can show that

T = (X<sub>n+1</sub> − X̄) / (S√(1 + 1/n))  ∼ t<sub>n−1</sub>

Therefore, Pr( X<sub>n+1</sub> ∈ X̄ ± t<sub>α/2, n−1</sub> ∙ S√(1 + 1/n) ) = 1 − α. PI for X<sub>n+1</sub> is

(X̄ − t<sub>α/2, n−1</sub> ∙ S√(1 + 1/n), X̄ + t<sub>α/2, n−1</sub> ∙ S√(1 + 1/n))

Comparison between CI and PI:

* CI is the interval estimation of E(X) = μ, PI is the interval estimation of X = [E(X) + randomness].
