**Normal Approximation to Binomial Distribution**

**Proposition**

Let X be a binomial rv based on n trials with success probability p. Then if the binomial probability histogram is not too skewed, X has approximately a normal distribution with 
µ<sub>X</sub> = np and σ<sub>X</sub> = √np(1 − p) = √npq.

**In particular, for x = a possible value of X,**

P(X ≤ x) = Bi(x; n, p) ≈ (area under the normal curve to the left of x + 0.5) = φ((x + 0.5 − np)/√npq)

**For practical purposes, n is large enough when both np ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) ≥ 10, the approximation is adequate, since there is then enough symmetry in the underlying binomial distribution.**

A direct proof of this result is quite difficult. In the next chapter we’ll see that it is a consequence of a more general result called the Central Limit Theorem.

In all honesty, this approximation is not so important for probability calculation as it once was. This is because software can now calculate binomial probabilities exactly for quite large values of n.

**Example 12.7** Suppose that 25% of all students at a large public university receive financial aid. Let X be the number of students in a random sample of size 50 who receive financial aid, so that p = 0.25. Then µ<sub>X</sub> = 12.5 and σ<sub>X</sub> = 3.06.

**Since np = 50(0.25) = 12.5 ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) = 37.5 ≥ 10, the approximation can safely be applied.**

The probability that at most 10 students receive aid is

P(X ≤ 10)
= Bi(10; 50, 0.25)
≈ φ((10 + 0.5 − 12.5)/√50(0.25)(0.75)) = φ(−0.65) = 0.2578.

The exact probabilities are 0.2622, so the approximations are quite good.
