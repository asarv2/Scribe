**Two sample confidence interval and hypotheses testing**

Previously, we only consider the inference about a single mean, or a single proportion. Now we extend the study to comparison between the means/proportions of two populations. For example, a hypothesis testing question:

𝐻0:μ1 = μ2  vs  𝐻𝑎: μ1 ≠μ2 
or 
𝐻0: 𝑝1 = 𝑝2  vs  𝐻𝑎:𝑝1 ≠𝑝2. 


**Matched pairs samples vs independent samples**

There are two ways to design a study to compare the means of 2 groups:

* **Independent samples** – Study in which subjects are randomly selected and measured or an experiment with a completely randomized design.

* **Matched Pairs Design** – Study in which subjects are somehow paired. (i.e. matched pair design, before and after studies in which each subject is measured twice – once before treatment and once after treatment).


**Example 24.1: Test if taking practice exam helps one type of learner more than another type.**

**Design 1 - Completely Randomized:**

* Randomly assign students to take MC practice exam (group 2) or no practice exam (group 1).
* Compare 2 groups.


**Definition: independent data**

The data consists of two group of samples, {𝑋1, … , 𝑋𝑚} and {𝑌1, … , 𝑌𝑛}. {𝑋1, … , 𝑋𝑚} is a random sample from a distribution with mean μ1 and variance σ1<sup>2</sup>; {𝑌1, … , 𝑌𝑛} is a random sample from a distribution with mean μ2 and variance σ2<sup>2</sup>; and X’s and Y’s are independent to each other.

**Two sample Z test/CI for mean comparison**

The inferences discussed in this section concern a difference 𝜇<sub>1</sub> – 𝜇<sub>2</sub> between the means of two population distributions.

* One such null hypothesis 𝐻<sub>0</sub> would state that

𝐻<sub>0</sub>: 𝜇<sub>1</sub> – 𝜇<sub>2</sub> = Δ<sub>0</sub> or 𝜇<sub>1</sub> = 𝜇<sub>2</sub> + Δ<sub>0</sub>

Three possible alternative hypotheses:

𝐻<sub>1</sub>: 𝜇<sub>1</sub> −𝜇<sub>2</sub> > 𝛥<sub>0</sub>
𝐻<sub>1</sub>: 𝜇<sub>1</sub> −𝜇<sub>2</sub> < 𝛥<sub>0</sub>
𝐻<sub>1</sub>: 𝜇<sub>1</sub> −𝜇<sub>2</sub> ≠𝛥<sub>0</sub>

* Alternatively, it may be appropriate to estimate 𝜇<sub>1</sub> – 𝜇<sub>2</sub> by computing a C level CI.


Basic Assumptions: independent data

1. 𝑋<sub>1</sub>, … , 𝑋<sub>𝑚</sub> is a random sample from a normal distribution with mean μ<sub>1</sub> and variance σ<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup>
2. 𝑌<sub>1</sub>, … , 𝑌<sub>𝑛</sub> is a random sample from another normal distribution with mean μ<sub>2</sub> and variance σ<sub>2</sub><sup>2</sup>
3. The X and Y samples are independent of one another.


Theorem 1:

* 𝑋̅ −𝑌̅ is a point estimator for 𝜇<sub>1</sub> – 𝜇<sub>2</sub>.
* 𝐸(𝑋̅ −𝑌̅) = μ<sub>1</sub> −μ<sub>2</sub>, 𝑋̅ −𝑌̅ is unbiased.
* 𝑉𝑎𝑟(𝑋̅ −𝑌̅) =  σ<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup>/𝑚 + σ<sub>2</sub><sup>2</sup>/𝑛.

* Furthermore,

o Case 1, if both X and Y have normal distributions, 𝑋̅ −𝑌̅ follows exactly normal distribution.

o Case 2, if X and Y are not normal, but both m and n are large enough, then 𝑋̅ −𝑌̅ follows approximately normal distribution.

Case 4: X and Y are normal with unknown with equal variances:
Pooled t procedure

If we can assume that not only that the two population distributions are normal but also that they have equal variances, we can use a pooled t test statistic for difference of the two samples.

Let σ<sup>2</sup> denote the common population variance. Then standardizing X̅ − Y̅ gives

Z<sub>TS</sub> = (X̅ − Y̅) − Δ<sub>0</sub>
√σ<sup>2</sup>/m + σ<sup>2</sup>/n  
= (X̅ − Y̅) − Δ<sub>0</sub>
σ√(1/m + 1/n)

which has a standard normal distribution.

The following weighted average of the two sample variances, called the pooled (i.e., combined) estimator of σ<sup>2</sup>, adjusts for any difference between the two sample sizes:

S<sub>p</sub><sup>2</sup> = ((m − 1)S<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup> + (n − 1)S<sub>2</sub><sup>2</sup>) / (m + n − 2)

Note: This estimator combines the information of data X and Y together for the estimation. The first sample contributes m − 1 df to the estimator, and the second sample contributes n − 1 df, for a total of m + n − 2 df.

This pooled estimator of σ<sup>2</sup> will be used to substitute unknown population variance σ<sup>2</sup> in the above expression for Z.

**Two sample paired test (paired data)**

By the definition of paired data, the problem of comparing two population means is equivalent to testing the population mean of D. Therefore, inference of paired data essentially is the inference of the one sample *D* data.

**Basic Assumptions: paired data**

# Slide 14

**Summary**

There are two populations and the corresponding two samples. To compare the two populations, we may simply compare the two population means, denoted as μ<sub>1</sub> and μ<sub>2</sub>.

Two scenarios:
* The two samples are independent.
* The two samples are paired.

We have learned two-sample t tests and CI for each of the two scenarios. When to use which depends on whether the two samples are independent or correlated.


Independent versus paired two-sample t procedures

* Because there is typically much less variability in the differences than in the x and y values, when data is paired,
    * The paired t CI will usually be narrower than the (incorrect) two-sample t CI.
    * Apply 2-sample t-test to paired data usually leads to smaller t statistics and larger p-value, thus make it difficult to detect the difference.

* In general, Paired experiment vs unpaired experiment (block vs non-block)
    * If there is great heterogeneity between experimental units and a large correlation within experimental units (large positive r), then the loss in degrees of freedom will be compensated for by the increased precision associated with pairing, so a paired experiment is preferable to an independent-samples experiment.
    * If the experimental units are relatively homogeneous and the correlation within pairs is not large, the gain in precision due to pairing will be outweighed by the decrease in degrees of freedom, so an independent-samples experiment should be used.
