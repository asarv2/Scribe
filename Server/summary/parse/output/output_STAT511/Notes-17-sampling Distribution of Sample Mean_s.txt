**Sampling Distribution of the Sample Mean**

Yuan Qu

The previous simulation studies show that when *n* is sufficiently large, the sampling distribution of sample variance and sample mean is more and more normal. Actually, most of the statistics that are based on average have this property. We particularly study the sampling distribution of sample mean in this section.

Proposition: Let X<sub>1</sub>, X<sub>2</sub>, ..., X<sub>n</sub> be a random sample from a distribution with mean value μ and standard deviation σ. Then

1. E(X̄) = μ<sub>X̄</sub> = μ
2. Var(X̄) = σ<sub>X</sub><sup>2</sup>/n and σ<sub>X̄</sub> = σ<sub>X</sub>/√n

In addition, with T = X<sub>1</sub> + X<sub>2</sub> + ... + X<sub>n</sub>, the sample total: E(T) = nμ<sub>X</sub>, Var(T) = nσ<sub>X</sub><sup>2</sup> and σ<sub>T</sub> = √nσ<sub>X</sub>.

The standard deviation σ<sub>X̄</sub> = σ<sub>X</sub>/√n is often called the standard error of the mean; it describes the magnitude of a typical or representative deviation of the sample mean from the population mean.

Proposition: Let X<sub>1</sub>, X<sub>2</sub>, ..., X<sub>n</sub> be a random sample from a normal distribution with mean μ and standard deviation σ. Then for any *n*, X̄ is normally distributed (with mean μ and standard deviation σ/√n, as is T (with mean nμ and standard deviation √nσ).

**A very important statistical result: Central Limit Theorem (CLT)**

Let X<sub>1</sub>, X<sub>2</sub>, ..., X<sub>n</sub> be a random sample from a distribution with mean μ and variance σ<sup>2</sup>. Then if n is sufficiently large, X̄ has approximately a normal distribution with μ<sub>X̄</sub> = μ<sub>X</sub> and σ<sub>X̄</sub><sup>2</sup> = σ<sub>X</sub><sup>2</sup>/n, and T also has approximately a normal distribution with mean nμ<sub>X</sub> and variance nσ<sub>X</sub><sup>2</sup>. The larger the value of n, the better the approximation.

Note: if the underlying distribution is close to a normal density curve, then the approximation will be good even for a small n, whereas if it is far from being normal, then a large n will be required.

**Recall the fact that we can use normal to approximate a Binomial distribution.**

Yuan Qu

The CLT can be used to justify the normal approximation to the binomial distribution discussed earlier.

We know that a binomial variable X is the number of successes in a binomial experiment consisting of n independent success/failure trials with p = P(S) for any particular trial. Define a new rv X<sub>1</sub> by

X<sub>1</sub> = { 1, if the 1st trial results in a success
         0, if the 1st trial results in a failure

and define X<sub>2</sub>, X<sub>3</sub>, ..., X<sub>n</sub> analogously for the other n–1 trials. Each X<sub>i</sub> indicates whether or not there is a success on the corresponding trial.

When the X<sub>i</sub>’s are summed, an 1 is added for every Success that occurs and a 0 for every Failure, so X<sub>1</sub> + ... + X<sub>n</sub> = X. The sample mean of the X<sub>i</sub>’s is p̂ = X/n, the sample proportion of successes. That is, both X and p̂ are approximately normal when n is large.


Example 17.4 A poll is conducted in order to learn about the amount of support a candidate (say for president) has. Suppose 50% of the population support the candidate. **Distribution of Linear Combination**

Yuan Qu

In this section, we consider the statistics: given fixed coefficients a<sub>i</sub>'s

Y = a<sub>1</sub>X<sub>1</sub> + ... + a<sub>n</sub>X<sub>n</sub>

Note sample mean is a special case where a<sub>i</sub> = 1/n.


Proposition: Let X<sub>i</sub> be a random variable with mean μ<sub>i</sub> and variance σ<sub>i</sub><sup>2</sup>. Then,

1. E(Y) = E(a<sub>1</sub>X<sub>1</sub> + ... + a<sub>n</sub>X<sub>n</sub>) = a<sub>1</sub>E(X<sub>1</sub>) + ... + a<sub>n</sub>E(X<sub>n</sub>) = a<sub>1</sub>μ<sub>1</sub> + ... + a<sub>n</sub>μ<sub>n</sub>.

2. If X<sub>i</sub> are mutually independent rv's, then

Var(Y) = a<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup>Var(X<sub>1</sub>) + ... + a<sub>n</sub><sup>2</sup>Var(X<sub>n</sub>) = a<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup>σ<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup> + ... + a<sub>n</sub><sup>2</sup>σ<sub>n</sub><sup>2</sup>.

3. In general

Var(a<sub>1</sub>X<sub>1</sub> + ... + a<sub>n</sub>X<sub>n</sub>) = Σ<sub>i=1</sub><sup>n</sup> Σ<sub>j=1</sub><sup>n</sup> a<sub>i</sub>a<sub>j</sub>Cov(X<sub>i</sub>, X<sub>j</sub>) = Σ<sub>i=1</sub><sup>n</sup> a<sub>i</sub><sup>2</sup>Var(X<sub>i</sub>) + 2 Σ<sub>i<j</sub> Cov(X<sub>i</sub>, X<sub>j</sub>).


Note: The above proposition concerns only mean and variance, but not the distribution. In general, the distribution shape of a linear combination is not easy to figure out.


Usually, a linear combination of normal variable is also a normal random variable. Since a normal distribution is completely determined by its mean and variance, we only need to figure out the distribution of a linear combination of normal rv's.


The difference between two random variables

An important special case of a linear combination results from taking n = 2, a<sub>1</sub> = 1, and a<sub>2</sub> = -1. We get the formula for the different of two rv's.

Proposition:

1. E(X<sub>1</sub> - X<sub>2</sub>) = E(X<sub>1</sub>) - E(X<sub>2</sub>) = μ<sub>1</sub> - μ<sub>2</sub> for any two rv's.

2. Var(X<sub>1</sub> - X<sub>2</sub>) = Var(X<sub>1</sub>) + Var(X<sub>2</sub>) = σ<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup> + σ<sub>2</sub><sup>2</sup> if X<sub>1</sub> and X<sub>2</sub> are independent rv's.
