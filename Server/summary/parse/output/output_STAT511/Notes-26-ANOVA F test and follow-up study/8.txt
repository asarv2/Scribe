**Statistical Methods**

**Multiple Comparison of treatments**

If the ANOVA F-test is not significant, stop. Then we claim the data provide no evidence in favor of 𝐻<sub>𝑎</sub>.

When 𝐻<sub>0</sub> is rejected, we claim there is difference among 𝐼 groups. Then a natural follow-up question: where are the differences? More specifically, among all 𝐼(𝐼−1)/2 possible pairwise comparisons, which are significantly different, which are not? A method for carrying out this further analysis is called a multiple comparisons procedure, as there are multiple pairs of differences to be compared.

For example, we calculate a confidence interval for each pairwise difference 𝜇<sub>𝑖</sub>−𝜇<sub>𝑗</sub>  with 𝑖<𝑗. If the interval for 𝜇<sub>1</sub> −𝜇<sub>2</sub> does not include 0, conclude that 𝜇<sub>1</sub> and 𝜇<sub>2</sub> differ significantly from one another; if the interval does include 0, the two 𝜇’s are judged not significantly different. The difficulty is how to control the simultaneous confidence level for all 𝐼(𝐼−1)/2 intervals. Equivalently, we can not perform several 2-sample t- or z-tests, since it fails to control the probability of overall type I error.


**The Bonferroni Method**

Suppose that we conduct 𝑘= 6 tests of hypotheses, each at 𝛼= 0.01. Then the overall risk of Type I error — that is, the chance of rejecting at least one of the 6 hypotheses when in fact all of them are true — cannot exceed 6 × 0.01 = 0.06.

Turning this logic around, if we want the overall risk of Type I error not to exceed 0.05, we will make each of the separate tests at a significance level of 0.05/6 = 0.0083. This is called a Bonferroni adjustment.
