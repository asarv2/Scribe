**Probability Cont.**

* Recall that events A<sub>1</sub>, A<sub>2</sub>, ..., A<sub>k</sub> are mutually exclusive if no two have any common outcomes.
* Exhaustive events are a set of events {A<sub>1</sub>, A<sub>2</sub>, ..., A<sub>k</sub>} in a sample space S such that one of event compulsorily occurs while performing the study, that is, (A<sub>1</sub> ∪... ∪A<sub>k</sub>) = S and one A<sub>i</sub> must occur.
    * Example: while tossing an unbiased coin, there are two possible outcomes - heads or tails. So, these two outcomes are exhaustive events as one of them will definitely occur while flipping the coin.

**The Law of Total Probability**

Let A<sub>1</sub>, A<sub>2</sub>, ..., A<sub>k</sub> be mutually exclusive and exhaustive events. Then for any other event B,
P(B) = P(A<sub>1</sub> ∩B) + ⋯+ P(A<sub>k</sub>∩B) = P(B|A<sub>1</sub>) × P(A<sub>1</sub>) + ⋯+ P(B|A<sub>k</sub>) × P(A<sub>k</sub>)

**Bayes’ Theorem**

* Used when: know P(B|A) but want P(A|B). For example, diagnostic tests provide P(Test+ | disease), but we are interested in P(disease | Test +).
* Bayes’ Theorem provides a method for finding P(A|B) from P(B|A).

**Bayes’ Theorem**

Let A<sub>1</sub>, A<sub>2</sub>, ..., A<sub>k</sub> be a set of k mutually exclusive and exhaustive events with prior probabilities P(A<sub>j</sub>), j = 1, ..., k. We often know the chance of another event B under each of the events A<sub>1</sub>, A<sub>2</sub>, ..., A<sub>k</sub>, i.e. P(B|A<sub>i</sub>). Then the posterior probability of A<sub>j</sub> given that B has occurred is
P(A<sub>i</sub>|B) = P(A<sub>i</sub>∩B) / P(B) = P(B|A<sub>i</sub>) × P(A<sub>i</sub>) / [P(B|A<sub>1</sub>) × P(A<sub>1</sub>)+...+P(B|A<sub>k</sub>) × P(A<sub>k</sub>)]

**Example 7.1**

An individual has 3 different email accounts. Most of her messages, in fact 70%, come into account #1, whereas 20% come into account #2 and the remaining 10% into account #3. Of the messages into account #1, only 1% are spam, whereas the corresponding percentages for accounts #2 and #3 are 2% and 5%, respectively.
Let A<sub>i</sub> = {message is from account #i} for i = 1, 2, 3; B = {message is spam}. Then the given percentages imply that
P(A<sub>1</sub>) = 0.70
P(A<sub>2</sub>) = 0.20
P(A<sub>3</sub>) = 0.10
P(B|A<sub>1</sub>) = 0.01
P(B|A<sub>2</sub>) = 0.02
P(B|A<sub>3</sub>) = 0.05

a) What is the probability that a randomly selected message is spam?
Use the law of total probability,
P(B) = P(B|A<sub>1</sub>) × P(A<sub>1</sub>) + P(B|A<sub>2</sub>) × P(A<sub>2</sub>) + P(B|A<sub>3</sub>) × P(A<sub>3</sub>)
