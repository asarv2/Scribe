QUESTION 1
Part A:
Prove that the intersection of two convex sets is also a convex set.
A. Let $C_1$ and $C_2$ be two convex sets. Let $x, y \in C_1 \cap C_2$. Then $x, y \in C_1$ and $x, y \in C_2$. Since $C_1$ and $C_2$ are convex, for any $t \in [0, 1]$, $tx + (1-t)y \in C_1$ and $tx + (1-t)y \in C_2$. Therefore, $tx + (1-t)y \in C_1 \cap C_2$, proving that $C_1 \cap C_2$ is convex.
B. The intersection of two convex sets is not necessarily convex. A counterexample can be constructed.
C. Convexity is only defined for sets in $\mathbb{R}^2$, so the intersection of two convex sets in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is not necessarily convex.
D. The proof requires the use of the Caratheodory theorem.
E. The statement is only true if the convex sets are bounded.

ANSWER: A

EXPLANATION:
A. This option provides a correct and concise proof.  The argument directly uses the definition of convexity for both sets and shows that the line segment connecting any two points in the intersection remains within the intersection.
B. This is incorrect. The intersection of convex sets is always convex.
C. This is incorrect. Convexity is defined for sets in $\mathbb{R}^n$ for any positive integer $n$.
D. This is incorrect. While the Caratheodory theorem is related to convex sets, it is not necessary to prove this specific statement.
E. This is incorrect. Boundedness is not a requirement for the intersection of convex sets to be convex.
