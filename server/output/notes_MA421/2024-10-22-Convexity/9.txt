\underline{\text{Carathéodory Theorem}} \\
\text{THEOREM 10.3. The convex hull } conv(S) \text{ of a set } S \text{ in } \mathbb{R}^m \text{ consists of all convex} \\
\text{combinations of } \underline{m+1} \text{ points from } S: \\
conv(S) = \left\{ z = \sum_{j=1}^{m+1} t_j z_j : z_j \in S \text{ and } t_j \ge 0 \text{ for all } j, \text{ and } \sum_j t_j = 1 \right\} \\
pf \quad conv(S) = \left\{ z = \sum_{j=1}^n t_j z_j : z_j \in S, t_j \ge 0, \sum_j t_j = 1 \right\} \\
\text{Consider the LP: } \max \quad c^T x \\
s.t. \quad Ax = z \\
\quad x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n = 1 \\
\quad x_i \ge 0 \\
A = \begin{bmatrix} z_1 & z_2 & \dots & z_n \end{bmatrix} \quad (z \text{ is given, fixed}) \\
\text{This is feasible, and hence has a basic opt. soln} \\
\text{with } m+1 \text{ basic variables.}
</LATEX>

<DESCRIPTION>This slide introduces Carathéodory's Theorem (Theorem 10.3) and begins its proof using linear programming. The theorem states that any point in the convex hull of a set $S$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$ can be expressed as a convex combination of at most $m+1$ points from $S$. The proof starts by representing the convex hull as the set of all convex combinations of points from $S$, where the number of points is not restricted. Then, a linear program (LP) is formulated to find a point $z$ in the convex hull. The LP's constraints ensure that a solution represents a convex combination of points from $S$. The objective function is unspecified, implying that the focus is on the feasibility and the structure of the optimal solution. The final statement asserts that the LP is feasible and has a basic optimal solution with at most $m+1$ basic variables, which directly implies that $z$ can be expressed as a convex combination of at most $m+1$ points from $S$, thus proving Carathéodory's Theorem.  The underlining highlights the key parts of the theorem and the proof. The notation "pf" stands for "proof," and "conv." is short for "convex". The context of linear programming is crucial because the proof leverages the properties of linear programs to establish a fundamental result about convex hulls, which are central to linear programming theory.</DESCRIPTION>
