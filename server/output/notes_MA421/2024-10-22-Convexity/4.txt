\underline{\text{Thm 10.1}} \quad S \text{ is convex } \iff \\
\text{it contains all conv. comb. of points in } S \\
pf \quad \implies \\
\underline{\text{Suppose } S \text{ is convex}} \\
n=2: \quad z_1, z_2 \in S \implies t_1 z_1 + t_2 z_2 \in S, \quad t_1, t_2 \ge 0 \\
\quad t_1 + t_2 = 1 \\
n=4: \quad z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4 \in S \\
t_1 z_1 + t_2 z_2 + t_3 z_3 + t_4 z_4 = (t_1 + t_2 + t_3) \left( \frac{t_1}{t_1 + t_2 + t_3} z_1 + \frac{t_2}{t_1 + t_2 + t_3} z_2 + \frac{t_3}{t_1 + t_2 + t_3} z_3 \right) + t_4 z_4 \\
(t_1 + t_2 + t_3) + t_4 = 1 \\
t_1 + t_2 + t_3 \ge 0, \quad t_4 \ge 0 \\
\implies t_1 z_1 + t_2 z_2 + t_3 z_3 + t_4 z_4 \in S
</LATEX>

<DESCRIPTION>This slide continues the proof of Theorem 10.1, which states that a set $S$ is convex if and only if it contains all convex combinations of its points.  This slide focuses on proving the implication: if $S$ is convex, then it contains all convex combinations of its points. The proof uses induction, but instead of showing the inductive step from n=3 to n=4 directly, it jumps from n=2 to n=4. The base case ($n=2$) is shown: if $z_1, z_2 \in S$, then any convex combination $t_1z_1 + t_2z_2$ (with $t_1, t_2 \ge 0$ and $t_1 + t_2 = 1$) is also in $S$ by the definition of convexity. The inductive step ($n=4$) is then demonstrated. A convex combination $t_1z_1 + t_2z_2 + t_3z_3 + t_4z_4$ is rewritten as a convex combination of a convex combination of $z_1, z_2, z_3$ and $z_4$. Since the inner convex combination is in $S$ (by the base case and an assumed inductive step from n=3), and the outer convex combination is also in $S$ (by the definition of convexity), the entire expression is in $S$.  The underlining highlights key assumptions and conclusions. The notation "pf" stands for "proof," and "conv. comb." is short for "convex combination." The context of linear programming is crucial because this theorem is fundamental to understanding the properties of feasible regions in linear programming problems, which are often convex sets. The implication arrow ($\implies$) indicates the direction of the proof.  Note that the inductive step is not fully rigorous, as it implicitly assumes the result holds for $n=3$ without explicitly proving it.</DESCRIPTION>
