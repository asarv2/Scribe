\textbf{Three Steps of Simplex}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item In the first part of the iteration, we imagine that the current feasible tree solution has been adopted by a transportation company. Because of the shipping costs, the market price of the commodity will vary with location: for example, oranges cost more in Alaska than they do in Florida. If the unit price is $y_i$ at a node $i$ and if the company ships the commodity along an arc $ij$, then it is fair to expect a unit price of $y_i + c_{ij}$ at the node $j$: a lower price would make shipping along the arc $ij$ wasteful, whereas a higher price would allow competitors to undersell the company at $j$ and still make a profit for themselves. Our first task is to determine a set of "fair prices" $y_1, y_2, \dots, y_n$ such that
    $$y_i + c_{ij} = y_j$$
    for each $ij \in T$.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Description:}

This slide details the first step of a three-step simplex algorithm, likely for a network flow problem.  It explains the economic rationale behind the algorithm.  The algorithm assumes a transportation company has a current feasible solution (a spanning tree).  The prices $y_i$ represent the "fair" market prices at each node $i$ in the network.  The key idea is that if the company ships a commodity along an arc $ij$, the price at node $j$ should be the price at node $i$ plus the cost of shipping along that arc ($c_{ij}$).  This step is crucial for determining the next iteration of the algorithm, as it helps identify potential improvements in the solution. The notation $T$ likely refers to the set of arcs in the spanning tree. The example of oranges in Alaska and Florida illustrates the concept of varying market prices based on location.  The overall goal is to find a set of prices that are consistent with the shipping costs and allow the company to maintain profitability.
